Do Smaller MLB Field Dimensions Actually Yield More Home Runs?

Picture this: it’s 2025, you’re watching your favorite MLB player at the plate during a game. The pitch comes, and that player hits a beauty, sailing the ball just slightly over the outfield wall for a walk-off home run. Now imagine this exact same scenario in a different ballpark, where the ball is hit again into the exact same spot on the field, but it lands a whole twenty feet in front of the outfield wall. How is this possible?

If you didn’t know, not all professional baseball fields are created equal. While it is still true that all bases must be 90 feet apart and the pitcher’s mound must be 60 feet, six inches away from home plate in every single MLB ballpark, everything changes once we get to that ballpark’s outfield dimensions and fence distances. This made me wonder, with all of these different dimensions and distances, do smaller MLB ballparks end up yielding more home runs? Seems like a no-brainer question, of course they do, right? With this post, I set out to find the definitive answer.

First we must look at what these distances actually are. Below are three charts that depict the shortest distances to each of the centerfield walls, right field foul poles and left field foul poles. This way, we can see what these distances are for ourselves. I will also highlight each of the top five ballparks in total home runs hit in the 2025 season in red.

It is important to note that baseballr takes the absolute longest distance a ball can travel inside the park from home as its centerfield distance, not the actual straightaway distance from home to the centerfield wall.

Interesting, right? As we can see, the top five ballparks in home runs hit in 2025, being Dodger Stadium, Oriole Park at Camden Yards, Angel Stadium, Sutter Health Park and Yankee Stadium respectively, are scattered all over the place. Let’s take, for example, the centerfield wall chart. We can see that Dodger Stadium and Angel Stadium are both in the top five for shortest distance, so case closed right? Well if we look again, both Yankee Stadium and Oriole Park at Camden Yards are both in the bottom third of the league, but are still top 5 in home runs hit.

You also may have noticed that Fenway Park, home of the Boston Red Sox, has the longest distance to the centerfield wall, but the shortest distance to both the right field and left field foul poles in all major league baseball. Yet, it is not in the top five ballparks in total home runs hit in 2025. Not even in the top 15. Interesting right? Let’s keep digging.

Now you may be thinking about additional ways this could be the case. For example, in the latter months of the 2025 baseball season, division races tighten, powerhouses start to separate themselves and teams try much harder to earn those coveted home runs. I thought the exact same way, and decided to look at the 2025 season on a month by month basis and look at cumulative home runs hit by month to see if there was a pattern.

Interesting again. We can see that Dodger Stadium separated itself from the league in June, and kept that streak going through the regular season. However, we once again see Fenway Park in the bottom third of the league in total home runs hit. Why is this? There may be one glaring answer you’re shouting at your screen, that being Fenway Park’s iconic “Green Monster” wall that stretches along the left field wall, standing at a monstrous 37 feet, 2 inches from ground level. That height is more than enough to turn would-be home runs into doubles, or even singles that just bounce right off of it and stay in play. But that only accounts for the left field side, what about the right field side? Wide open for the right-field friendly hitters. PNC Park, home of the Pittsburgh Pirates as highlighted in the chart, has the fifth shortest average fence distance in the MLB, yet finds itself at the bottom of the league by a wide margin.

Now you may be thinking to yourself that the case is cut and dry now, right? Smaller MLB ballparks don’t actually yield more home runs. I still wasn’t convinced at this point, and decided to make one final graph to once and for all, finally determine the overall truth. To do this was rather simple. Just compare average fence distances to overall home runs hit in each ballpark, right? Absolutely right, so let’s take a look.

And there you have it. The final, definitive answer that smaller MLB ballbarks do not in fact, yield more home runs. If that were the case, you would expect that the further the average fence distance, the less home runs would be hit, and that green trendline would go downwards. The opposite happened. Albeit slightly, the trendline actually starts lower and trends upward. Outliers like Coors Field and Wrigley Field, homes of the Colorado Rockies and Chicago Cubs respectively, have the two longest average fence distances and still find themselves in the top third in the league in home runs hit in 2025. As stated earlier, Fenway Park has the lowest average outfield wall distance, yet finds itself in the bottom third of the league along with PNC Park. Dodger Stadium finds itself tied for the eighth shortest outfield wall distance, and yet, still dominated in the home runs hit category.

Now that we know smaller MLB ballparks don’t yield more home runs, we know now that it is just great players who know how to hit the ball well wherever they play.
